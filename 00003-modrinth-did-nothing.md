**Hi everyone,** G_cat here, the internet's busiest modded Minecraft drama nerd.

A couple of days ago as of the writing of this post, Modrinth, the second most popular platform for sharing mods, announced [through a blogpost](https://modrinth.com/news/article/russian-censorship/) that they were pressured by the Russian government to remove projects that contains mentions of LGBTQ+ content from their website or force potential blockage of *the entire site* on the territory of Russia. Their decision to comply with this request has angered multiple creators on the platform, causing some of them to take their projects down from Modrinth entirely.

# First time?

*If you don't know,* this practice of banning websites the Russian government doesn't like isn't something new. It has been used ever since the internet has started becoming a big tool for the opposition to voice their concerns, and has really started growing in use since *you know what event.* The government agency for this - *RosComNadzor* has already blocked things like *Twitter*, *Facebook*, and even *Discord* (which was up until its ban was used by some sectors of the goverment itself, lol).

Probably the most infamous example of this is [the partial ban of *YouTube*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=71DyCDqYUvI). Its Russian userbase is **so big**, that when it was still fully available, the Russian-speaking section of YouTube was the second most prioritised market for Google. Unlike Russian Twitter or Facebook, the majority of it wasn't political, so it couldn't have been easily taken down without any public uproar. That's why a different approach was taken to it, slowing down the website more and more each day until it became practically unusable, utilizing the [*boiling frog*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_frog) method. Technically, YouTube is still not fully blocked here, it all depends on the network you are on; but for me, usually at least the main page is able to load.

*However*, not fully blocking YouTube didn't convice the general public not to be mad. Even after trying to put the blame on Google themselves for "not maintaining the Russian portion of their cache servers" (*which is complete bullshit, servers don't turn into unservicable bricks in a night*), nobody believed them. People, even those not connected to politics in the slightest, didn't just accept the decision and moved on to more goverment-friendly alternatives. No, in a move that was the definition of silent protest, we've learned to overcome these artificial obsticles and continue enjoying our freedoms as we used to. **Which bring us to...**

# Improvise, adapt, overcome

After the attempted censorship of YouTube, a new market has emerged in the country - the *beautiful* world of *VPNs.* A couple of different Russian-centric proxy services has started appearing and quickly booming in popularity. They used protocols such as [**Shadowsocks**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadowsocks) or **VLESS**, which made them practically unblockable (unlike some popular western VPNs, which use pretty primitive protocols in terms of ban-resistance such as OpenVPN, see [*Surfshark* getting blocked](https://habr.com/ru/news/704810/)).

Russian government's practices of blocking websites for anything and everything, be it "radical extremist oppositional propaganda" or the very [historically-uncharacteristic](https://youtu.be/6iPiNUAmNwU) American-right-wing inspired anti-LGBT practics, have now amounted to nothing; and the digital libertarianism that our internet used to have during its inception was partically fully restored. **VPNs were not just for the politically dissident now,** having one was mandatory for any self-respecting internet user here. Banning resources did the opposite of the effect that the government tried to achieve, it radicalised those who were previously completely apolitical, forcing them to find a way to assert and regain their freedom, even if only online.

*Recently* I was asked what percentage of the people I knew IRL used a VPN. *I thought about it for a bit*, and I think the answer is **about 99.9%**, and that .1% is only due to my grandma, who doesn't use the internet at all (*shout out to her :)*). Not all of these 99.9% are super tech-savvy or liberal or anything, they are just regular people who don't want their freedoms to be cut short. Interpolated to the entire population, I think VPN users make up about 2/3rds or the entirety of the country, and that number only goes up if you are only looking at chronically online people which modded players tend to be. You probably know where I'm going with this.

# Nothing ever happens

**Modinth's decision** to remove the "offending" mods off of their platforms ***FOR RUSSIAN USERS ONLY*** (I see a lot of misconceptions about this, they were not going to remove them for everyone) would've most likely worked on a IP-location basis, figuring out which country your in based on your IP address. Combining this knowledge with everything I've said previously leads to the following, sort of obvious for me as a lifelong Russian citizen, but a little less obvious for people who haven't been living in hell for the past 3.5 years conclusion:

> *The thing Modrinth did to save their website from being blocked here*, did nothing to noone, and the consequences of not doing so would've also affected noone.

The amount of Russian modded players who don't use a VPN in the year 2025 can be rounded down to 0 as an inconsequential statistical oddity. The audience that the decision was meant to affect wouldn't have been affected either way, but the fact that the decision did go through lead to a intereting side effect of the western creator audience *pulling out their projects off of Modrinth* as a way to protest this decision. I am not here to diss anyone, and you shouldn't go harass any of the developers (*if for some reason that's the idea you are getting out of this*); it's just that for me, this is such a mind-numbingly useless way of trying to protest inequality and homophobia.

You are trying to hurt a company that has *previously been known to support LGBTQ creators and users* in every way they can, literally building part of their brand identity around this idea, making a decision that will, and I repeat, *affect noone in no way shape or form.* Even if the things I've said before weren't true, and the removal of said did actually affect every single Russian user, **I still don't see how this would've been useful to protest** - it is clear that the team behind Modrinth are not homophobes, and are purely doing this because they felt their hand was forced, not because of some evil intention. It's probably also important to mention that *only four mods* were supposed to be affected by this; I am not using that as an argument here because that doesn't mean more mods wouldn't have been blocked in the future, but still thought that was something I'd say (like, you are already protesting because of just four mods, could've at least waited to see if this practice would go further or just stop at one random goverment decision).

*I'm not here to force you to put your mods back on Modrinth,* and to be fair, I am not here to leave them completely blameless. I think that from the point of view of the Modrinth team, they should've seen the backlash coming, with how close to heart most of their userbase takes these issues (and justifiably so). If they had analysed the situation more thoroughly like I did here, they would've probably came to the conclusion that it's not worth getting such a reputation hit for nothing in return. *I would've evaluated the situation differently* if not following Russian law for a western company such as Modrinth could've came with worse consequenses then getting your site blocked - I probably would've been more protective of the decision, a small company going against an entire government usually does not end well for the company. But since in reality getting your website blocked from nobody isn't really a punishment of any severity, my point still stands.
